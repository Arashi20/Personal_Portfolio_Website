# Becoming a TalentPooler at De Goudse

In February, I wrote about the long and sometimes hopeless search for a junior role in data and AI: the endless vacancies asking for three years of experience, the 200+ applicants per position, and the KLM traineeship that ended just before the interviews. I ended that post with "What's next?". Well, here is the answer: I am now a **Data Engineer Trainee** at **De Goudse**, and I have just finished my first month.

## Finding the vacancy

There was no magic trick. I simply kept looking for AI and data vacancies across every job board and company website I could find, until I came across a traineeship in the **Data & AI** department of De Goudse. Trainees there are called **"TalentPoolers"**, and the role described a lot of opportunities in data engineering, data science and AI engineering. That matched both my previous work experience and the personal projects I had been building on the side, so I applied right away in April.

## The selection process

The procedure consisted of four steps:

1. **A short call with an external recruiter**, mostly to check the basics.
2. **An in-person interview with the head of HR.** This one was pretty chill. We talked about my personal life, who I am, what drives me, and what I want to become.
3. **An in-person interview with the team manager of the ART Data and a Product Manager.** This was the more technical round. They asked about my experience with data and AI, and I finally got the chance to ask all my questions about the programme and the department.
4. **An online assessment** measuring analytical skills. I scored above average, which I was secretly quite proud of.

Compared to the KLM procedure, what I liked most was that the **human component came early**. Instead of only being a score in a series of online tests, I got to show who I am from the second round onwards.

## Insurance? Really?

To be honest, I never expected to work for an insurance company. In my head, insurance was a stale business with little innovation and not much variety in the work. A month in, I can say I was wrong, at least about this department.

The insurance world is **a world full of data**, and full of complex questions about that data. That is exactly what makes it interesting: there is a lot of room for solid data engineering, innovative data science and AI solutions that are genuinely valuable to the business, and therefore to the customers.

## Why this role

Before De Goudse, I also received offers from a few small startups for roles as an "AI developer". When I looked closer, most of the work was still traditional software development, which did not really excite me. This role was different: it is a role I actually wanted, in a field I know, and one that fits me well. After such a long job hunt, getting the offer was a **huge relief**, and above all, I was just really happy.

## The traineeship

The traineeship lasts about **three years**. Every year, I join a different team within the ART Data & AI, for example in data engineering, business intelligence, data science or RPA. In each team I work on a project that broadens my skillset or deepens my knowledge of De Goudse's side of the insurance world.

For my first year, I am part of the **Data Enablement Team (DET)**, where we are building De Goudse's new data platform in **Microsoft Fabric**. My work focuses on delegated authority (volmacht) data: analyzing and modeling it, validating datasets with **(T-)SQL** and **PySpark**, flagging data quality issues, and documenting how everything fits together. It is a great place to start, because every data science or AI solution later on depends on a reliable data foundation.

## What's next?

One month in, I am mostly learning: about Fabric, about the insurance domain, and about how a data platform is built from the ground up. In the coming years, I hope to rotate into teams that do more data science and AI work, possibly including a team that works a lot in **R**, which will be a nice reunion with my bachelor's years.

To anyone still stuck in the job hunt I described in February: keep building, keep applying, and don't rule out industries you think are boring. Sometimes the most interesting data lives in the least expected places.
